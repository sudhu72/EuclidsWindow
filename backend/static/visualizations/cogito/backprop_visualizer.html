Compute Once, Reuse Backward

A forward pass turns inputs into a loss. A backward pass turns that loss back into "which way should each weight move" — and the whole algorithm is just the chain rule noticing that its middle term is shared. Step through both directions, one gear at a time.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

The Exact Worked Example, Made Walkable

What's happening: the tutorial's own numbers — x → a=w1·x → b=w2·a → L=(b−y)². Step forward through the teal arrows to compute a, b, and L. Then step backward through the red arrows: the loss hands back one number, δ_b, and that same number gets reused to compute both weight gradients — the whole "aha" of backpropagation, happening in front of you.

Try this: step all the way forward, then step backward one click at a time and watch δ_b appear once and get tapped twice — once to build dL/dw2, once to build δ_a (which then builds dL/dw1). Nothing about the shared error signal is ever recomputed.

forward pass backward pass (delta flow)
Why it matters: in a network with millions of weights, this "compute the shared middle term once, reuse it everywhere it feeds into" trick is the entire difference between training being feasible and being computationally hopeless.

One More Layer — Watch δ Cascade

What's happening: the identical idea, one layer deeper: x₀ → x₁ → x₂ → x₃ → L. Now there are three deltas instead of one, and each is built from the *next* delta — δ₂ is computed once and reused for both dL/dw3 and to build δ₁, and δ₁ is reused the same way one layer further back.

Try this: step all the way through and count: three weight gradients came out, but only three deltas were ever computed, each one shared by exactly the two things downstream of it — no gradient anywhere recomputed a delta that already existed.

Why it matters: stack this pattern to a hundred layers and the arithmetic per layer never changes — one multiply to get this layer's gradient, one multiply to hand the delta further back. That constant per-layer cost, not the depth, is why deep networks are trainable at all.
