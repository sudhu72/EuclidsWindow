Running Totals, Made Visible

A list that settles or runs away, an infinite sum that closes a paradox, two similarly-shrinking series racing to opposite fates, a boundary where a power series simply stops meaning anything, and a function's coefficients pinned down one derivative at a time. Each one built from a picture, not asserted.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Settle Down, Explode, or Oscillate Forever

What's happening: pick a rule and watch the terms plot themselves, one by one. Some sequences home in on a single value; others run away; others never stop flipping back and forth.

Try this: compare 1/n (settles at 0) with n (runs away) and (-1)ⁿ (oscillates forever, never settling) — three genuinely different fates, side by side.

Why it matters: "does this settle down?" is the same question whether it's a sequence of numbers, a running average (Law of Large Numbers), or a Markov chain's belief converging on a stationary distribution.

The Wall the Running Total Never Crosses

What's happening: each step covers a fixed fraction of what's left. Watch the running total climb toward a wall it approaches forever but never crosses — Zeno's paradox, resolved.

Try this: drag the ratio r and watch the formula a/(1−r) predict exactly where the wall sits, matching the climbing bars every time.

Why it matters: a bouncing ball, a decaying drug dose, a depreciating asset — anything that shrinks by a fixed fraction forever has a finite total, given by this exact formula.

Two Shrinking Series, Two Different Fates

What's happening: 1+1/2+1/3+... (harmonic, teal) and 1+1/4+1/9+... (Basel, gold) both have terms shrinking to zero. Watch their running totals race — one keeps climbing forever, agonizingly slowly; the other flattens onto a ceiling.

Try this: run to a few thousand terms and watch the harmonic total keep inching upward with no ceiling in sight, while the Basel total has already all-but-frozen near π²/6 ≈ 1.645.

Why it matters: "the terms shrink to zero" is necessary for convergence but nowhere near sufficient — the harmonic series is the permanent counterexample to keep in mind.

A Boundary You Can Actually Watch

What's happening: 1 + x + x² + x³ + ... is the geometric series with ratio x instead of r. Pick x and watch the partial sums either settle onto 1/(1−x) or run away — depending purely on which side of x = ±1 you're on.

Try this: set x=0.9 (settles, slowly) then x=1.1 (explodes immediately) — the radius of convergence isn't a vague idea, it's this exact, sharp boundary.

0.6
Why it matters: every power series — including every Taylor series — has a radius like this one, and a Taylor approximation is only trustworthy inside it.

Pin Down Each Coefficient, One Derivative at a Time

What's happening: assume f(x) = c₀+c₁x+c₂x²+... and match derivatives at x=0 one at a time. Each step reveals exactly one more coefficient — no guessing, just cₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!.

Try this: pick eˣ and step through — every coefficient becomes 1/n!, because every derivative of eˣ at 0 is just 1. Then try sin x and watch every other coefficient vanish, because sin's even derivatives at 0 are all zero.

Why it matters: this is the same series the Calculus visualizer's "Taylor Tower" tab shows hugging the true curve — that tool shows the geometric payoff; this one shows the algebraic reason each coefficient is exactly what it is.
