Graph Theory, Made Visible

Eight verified demos on the same "bowtie" graph and the real Königsberg bridges: classify a walk as you build it, watch Euler's degree-parity theorem decide a 300-year-old puzzle live, assemble a valid spanning tree, color a graph without conflicts, watch a matrix quietly count walks, step through Dijkstra's shortest paths, build the cheapest spanning tree with Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms, and handle negative edges and all-pairs distances with Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall.

Click Vertices to Build a Sequence

What's happening: click vertices, in order, on the "bowtie" graph (two triangles sharing vertex C). Every click extends the sequence; the live verdict below classifies it as a walk, trail, path, circuit, or cycle, exactly by the Part 2 rules.

Try this: click A → B → A → C and watch it register as "walk only" (edge A-B reused). Reset, then click A → B → C → D → E → C → A and watch it register as a circuit that happens to use every edge — an Eulerian circuit, live.

Why it matters: all five words describe the same kind of thing — a sequence of real edges — and differ only in what's allowed to repeat. Watching one sequence get built click by click is the fastest way to stop blurring them together.

The Actual 1736 Puzzle, With a Live Verdict

What's happening: four landmasses, seven bridges, drawn both as a map and as the graph Euler reduced it to. Toggle any bridge off and watch the degree sequence — and the Eulerian verdict — update live.

Try this: with all 7 bridges present, confirm the verdict is "impossible" (4 odd-degree vertices). Now click to remove just one bridge between A and B — watch the odd-vertex count drop to 2, and the verdict flip to "an Eulerian path now exists."

Why it matters: this is the literal 1736 puzzle, not a simplified stand-in — click any bridge to see the exact theorem that proved the walk impossible, and exactly how many bridges would need removing to make it solvable.

Select Edges Until You've Built a Valid Spanning Tree

What's happening: click edges of the bowtie graph to select or deselect them. The live check confirms whether your current selection is a valid spanning tree — exactly 4 edges, touching all 5 vertices, connected, with no cycle.

Try this: select all 6 edges and watch it fail ("too many edges, contains a cycle"). Drop the A-C edge and the C-D edge (one from each triangle) and watch it turn valid. There are exactly 9 such valid selections in this graph — try finding a second one.

Why it matters: a spanning tree is "just enough edges to stay connected, no more" — the "Show a valid spanning tree" button cycles through all 9 that exist in this graph, confirming the tutorial's verified count directly.

Color Every Vertex So No Edge Connects Two of the Same Color

What's happening: pick a preset graph, click a vertex, then click a color swatch to assign it. Any edge connecting two same-colored vertices turns red — a conflict.

Try this: pick the "5-cycle" preset and try to 2-color it — you'll always end up with exactly one conflicting edge, no matter how you alternate. Switch to "4-cycle" and 2-coloring works perfectly, confirming Part 6's even-vs-odd cycle rule live.

Why it matters: the chromatic number isn't guessed — it's whatever the smallest color count is that produces zero red edges, checked live, the same way exam timetables and CPU register allocators are solved for real.

A Matrix, Quietly Counting Walks

What's happening: the bowtie graph's adjacency matrix, raised to a power k. Click any two vertices to highlight that matrix entry and read off exactly how many length-k walks connect them.

Try this: set k=2, click A twice (A to A) and confirm the entry reads 2 — matching A's own degree exactly. Then click A then D and confirm it reads 1 — the single walk A→C→D.

Why it matters: matrix multiplication was invented for entirely different reasons, yet raising an adjacency matrix to the k-th power always, automatically, counts every walk of length k between every pair of vertices.

Step Through the Priority Queue, One Pop at a Time

What's happening: the same bowtie graph, now weighted. Pick a source vertex, then click "Step" to pop the currently-cheapest not-yet-finalized vertex, relax its neighbors, and watch the tentative-distance table update live.

Try this: from source A, step all the way through and watch E's tentative distance actually improve mid-run (11 → 10) the step after D gets popped — proof that "first discovered" and "final" are not the same claim.

Pick a source and click Step to begin.
Why it matters: the moment a vertex is popped with the smallest tentative distance remaining, no unexplored detour could ever beat it — because every edge weight is non-negative. That's the entire correctness argument, made watchable one pop at a time.

Kruskal and Prim, One Edge at a Time

What's happening: the same weighted bowtie graph as the Dijkstra tab (A-B=2, A-C=5, B-C=3, C-D=1, D-E=4, E-C=6). Each preset button plays the worked example from Part 4C: the edge being considered turns gold, then green if it's added to the tree or red-dashed if it's rejected, with the reason shown in the log. The running total and (for Kruskal) the Union-Find groups update after every step.

Try this: click "Kruskal: run all" and confirm the total is 10, with A-C and E-C rejected because their ends were already connected. Then click "Prim: run all" and confirm Prim finds the same four edges and the same total, 10. Finally click "Show all 9 spanning trees" to check that 10 really is the cheapest.

Pick a preset above to begin.
Why it matters: both algorithms are greedy, yet both land on the same cheapest tree that brute force finds. Kruskal never rejects an edge for its price, only because its two ends are already joined (it would close a loop). That's the cut property, visible one edge at a time.

Negative Edges: Relax Everything, V − 1 Times

What's happening: every worked example from Part 4D is a one-click preset that plays itself. Step moves one edge (or one round, or one stopover) at a time, Play animates, Jump to end shows the answer. Negative edge weights are drawn in red; the number under each vertex is its current distance from the source.

Try this: click "Dijkstra trips on a negative edge" and watch A get finalized at 2 one step too early. Then click "Bellman-Ford on the same graph" and watch the round-by-round relaxation get A = 1. "Worst edge order" shows why V − 1 rounds can really be needed; "Negative cycle" shows the extra round that catches one.

Why it matters: Dijkstra's speed comes from trusting a vertex the moment it's popped, and a negative edge is exactly what breaks that trust. Bellman-Ford trusts nothing: it re-checks every edge, every round, which is slower (O(V·E)) but correct with negative weights, and it can tell you when no answer exists.
