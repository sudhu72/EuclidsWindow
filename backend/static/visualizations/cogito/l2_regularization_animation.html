Why squared, and not just penalizing size directly? Try it in your own body: carry a 20kg bag split 10kg in each hand, versus all 20kg in one hand. The second one hurts far more than "twice as much" — strain scales roughly with load squared, not load. Check the arithmetic: 10² + 10² = 200, but 20² + 0² = 400 — identical total weight, yet concentrating it into one arm doubles the "pain."

That's exactly what squaring does to a network's weights. For a fixed amount of total "reliance" the model needs to place somewhere, concentrating it into one giant weight costs far more than spreading the same total thinly across many small weights — even though the sum is identical. A model leaning almost entirely on one weight is carrying the groceries in one arm: it works, but it's fragile. Spreading the same predictive power across many small weights is carrying two lighter bags: same capability, far less strain, no single point of failure.

What does λ actually do? It's the exchange rate between "fitting the data well" and "staying spread out." λ = 0 is no regularization at all — back to the overfitting risk from Part 1.5. A huge λ forces every weight toward 0, starving the model into underfitting. Try dragging the slider in the diagram above and watch the gap between the two configurations grow as λ increases — the penalty for concentrating gets steeper and steeper.
