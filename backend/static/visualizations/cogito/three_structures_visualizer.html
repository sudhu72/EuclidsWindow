The Strip With Only One Side

What's happening: a Möbius strip is a paper loop with a half-twist before the ends are joined. Cut a normal loop and you always have two distinct faces; give it a half-twist first, and there's only one continuous surface. Here, "Lane A" and "Lane B" both represent that same surface, and the wrap between them is where the half-twist lives.

Try this: press Walk the strip and watch a lone ant walk forward, never once crossing an edge or lifting its pen. After crossing the twist once, it's on "the other side" — after crossing it twice, it's back exactly where it started. It walked the entire surface without ever needing to flip over, because there was only ever one surface to walk.

Why it matters: this is the single most counterintuitive claim in topology, made walkable instead of taken on faith — cut an actual strip of paper with a half-twist and try it by hand if you have one, and this diagram will match exactly what you feel happen.

Homology: Counting Holes

β₀ = pieces, β₁ = loops that can't shrink away, β₂ = enclosed cavities. The page builds each shape's boundary matrices and computes βₖ = (k-pieces) − rank(out) − rank(in) with its own Gaussian elimination. Persistent-homology presets grow the radius ε step by step.

Where this connects: combinations correct for overcounting the same way Jaccard similarity's union does. The order-dependent square example above is literally the Rubik's Cube's structure, miniaturized. The Euler characteristic is a simple count that happens to detect genuine topological information — dragging the blob changes its geometry constantly but never its genus, exactly as stretching a coffee mug into a donut never changes its one hole.
