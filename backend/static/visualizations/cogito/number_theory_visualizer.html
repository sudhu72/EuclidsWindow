The "Musical Chairs" Proof, Made Visible

What's happening: pick a prime p and a multiplier a. The top row is the chairs 1, 2, ..., p−1 in order; the bottom row is what happens when you multiply every chair by a, mod p. The lines show where each chair lands.

Try this: for any prime p and any a not divisible by it, notice the bottom row is always a reshuffle of the exact same numbers — no chair vanishes, none repeats, none is new. That's the whole proof: since multiplying just permutes the chairs, the product of all of them can't change, and cancelling that shared product from both sides leaves a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p) — verified live below.

Why it matters: this "same set, different order" argument — a genuine symmetry of the chair set — is the entire content of Fermat's Little Theorem, and it's what RSA's decrypt-undoes-encrypt guarantee is ultimately built on (next tab).

Encrypt, Then Decrypt — Watch It Actually Work

What's happening: the exact toy RSA recipe from the tutorial — primes p=3, q=11, so n=33 and φ(n)=20. Pick a public exponent e; its private partner d is computed automatically so that e×d ≡ 1 (mod 20).

Try this: slide the message m and watch it get scrambled into a ciphertext c by the public key, then unscrambled back to the exact original by the private key — every single time, for every message and every valid e you pick. That reliability is Fermat's theorem, at work behind the scenes.

Why it matters: factoring n=33 back into 3 × 11 is trivial at this toy size — real RSA is this identical four-step recipe with p and q hundreds of digits long, where factoring n back apart is the computationally infeasible step that keeps the private key private.

Where this connects: the clock here is the same repeating cyclic structure from group theory, now with multiplication added — watch how a prime modulus makes every number invertible at once. The Euclidean Algorithm's "shrink without losing the answer" move echoes the recursion in Pascal's Triangle (combinatorics). The sieve is the counting machinery underneath the primes that Fermat's Little Theorem and RSA both depend on.
