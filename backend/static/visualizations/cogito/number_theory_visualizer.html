The "Musical Chairs" Proof, Made Visible

What's happening: pick a prime p and a multiplier a. The top row is the chairs 1, 2, ..., p−1 in order; the bottom row is what happens when you multiply every chair by a, mod p. The lines show where each chair lands.

Try this: for any prime p and any a not divisible by it, notice the bottom row is always a reshuffle of the exact same numbers — no chair vanishes, none repeats, none is new. That's the whole proof: since multiplying just permutes the chairs, the product of all of them can't change, and cancelling that shared product from both sides leaves a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p) — verified live below.

Euler's Theorem — Why RSA Decryption Actually Works

What's happening: Fermat's a^(p−1) ≡ 1 only works when the modulus is prime. RSA's modulus n = 33 = 3 × 11 is not. Euler's fix: use φ(n) instead of n − 1, so a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) for every a sharing no factor with n. Here φ(33) = 20.

Try this: click any button below. Each one plays a fully worked example from the tutorial, every step computed and shown line by line — nothing to work out by hand. Powers are computed by repeated squaring, so you can see each intermediate remainder.

Why it matters: RSA picks keys with e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) so the combined exponent is "a multiple of φ(n), plus one" — and Euler's theorem erases the multiple. Computing φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) needs the secret primes, which is exactly the trapdoor.

Which Remainders Are Actually Squares?

What's happening: pick a prime p and watch every nonzero remainder get squared mod p. Quadratic residues (green) are the results that actually show up; non-residues (red outline) never appear as anyone's square.

Why it matters: squaring is always exactly 2-to-1 on the nonzero remainders, so exactly half ever show up as a square, for any odd prime — Euler's criterion tells you which half without squaring anything.

Where this connects: the clock here is the same repeating cyclic structure from group theory, now with multiplication added — watch how a prime modulus makes every number invertible at once. The sieve is the counting machinery underneath the primes that Fermat's Little Theorem and RSA both depend on.
