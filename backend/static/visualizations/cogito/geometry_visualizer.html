Shapes, Rearranged

Six facts everyone learns and almost nobody watches happen: why a transversal stamps the same angle pattern twice, why a triangle's angles are forced to 180°, why the same four triangles prove a²+b²=c² two different ways, why a triangle/parallelogram/trapezoid's area formula is really just a rectangle in disguise, why a sliced pizza becomes a rectangle, and why elephants need such thick legs. Each one built from a picture, not asserted.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

The Stamped-Copy Pattern

What's happening: two parallel lines, cut by one transversal, create an identical angle pattern at each crossing — as if a rubber stamp hit the page twice. Drag the transversal's angle and watch every one of the eight angles update together.

Try this: pick each relationship button and watch which pair of angles highlights — corresponding angles sit in matching positions and are always equal; alternate interior angles sit on opposite sides of the transversal, between the lines, and are also always equal; co-interior angles sit on the same side, between the lines, and always add to 180°.

Why it matters: these three facts, chained together, are the entire raw material the triangle angle-sum proof (next tab) assembles into a single straight line.

Watch the Angle-Sum Assemble Itself

What's happening: drag the apex of the triangle. A construction line, parallel to the base, is drawn through the apex — and by the transversal facts from the previous tab, the two base angles get "relocated" up to the apex, sitting right next to its own angle, all three visibly forming one straight line: 180°.

Try this: drag the apex into a tall, skinny triangle, then a short, wide one — the three relocated angles keep assembling into a straight line every time, for every shape you can make.

Thales's Trick: Scale It and Watch the Angles Freeze

What's happening: scale the same triangle up or down from its left vertex. Every side length changes — but every angle stays frozen, because scaling never touches shape, only size. That frozen-angle, scaled-sides pattern is exactly what Thales used to measure the pyramid.

1.00
Why it matters: "same angles, scaled sides" is the entire content of similarity — and it's a free, exact ratio the moment you can guarantee two triangles share their angles, no protractor required in the field.

Same Four Triangles, Two Arrangements, One Leftover Area

What's happening: a square of side (a+b) always holds the same four right triangles (legs a, b) no matter how you place them inside it — so whatever's left over has to have the identical total area, however differently-shaped the leftover looks.

Try this: toggle between arrangements and watch the leftover space snap from one tilted square (area c²) into two upright squares (areas a² and b²) — the readout confirms both leftovers are exactly the same number, for any a and b you choose.

Why it matters: this is area conservation doing all the work — nobody had to assume a²+b²=c², it fell out of refusing to let the same square's total area change just because you rearranged what's inside it.

Cut, Rotate, or Slide — the Area Never Changes

What's happening: pick which rearrangement to watch, then drag the "Rearrange" slider from 0 (the original shape) to 1 (the target shape) — the moving piece is always a rigid copy, just rotated or slid into place, so its own area can never change no matter where it ends up.

Try this: drag base/height (and, for the trapezoid, the two parallel sides) to any values you like, then sweep "Rearrange" back and forth — the total-area readout never moves, confirming ½×base×height, base×height, and ½×(a+b)×h aren't rules to memorize, just this same cut-and-slide fact stated three ways.

Slicing a Pizza Until It's a Rectangle

What's happening: the circle on the left is sliced into equal wedges. On the right, those same wedges are laid out alternating tip-up, tip-down — interlocking into a shape that hugs closer and closer to a true rectangle, width πr, height r, as the wedge count climbs.

Try this: drag the wedge count from a chunky 4 up to 100 and watch the right-hand shape's zigzag edges smooth into straight lines, while the area readout locks onto πr².

12
Why it matters: the only approximation here is treating each wedge's curved outer edge as straight — and that error shrinks to nothing as the wedges get thinner, which is the exact same limiting idea calculus later formalizes as a Riemann sum.

Length, Area, and Volume Race Apart

What's happening: scale every dimension of a shape by k. Length scales by k, area by k², volume by k³ — three different exponents racing apart the moment k moves away from 1.

Try this: push k up to 2 and watch length merely double while volume rockets to 8× — then watch the strength-to-weight bar (∝1/k) shrink, the exact reason a scaled-up mouse would collapse under its own re-sized bones.

1.00
Length ∝ k
Area ∝ k²
Volume ∝ k³
Strength ÷ weight ∝ 1/k
Why it matters: this single mismatch of exponents is why elephants have disproportionately thick legs, why shrews must eat almost constantly, and why a scaled-up bridge model needs far more than proportionally thicker beams.
