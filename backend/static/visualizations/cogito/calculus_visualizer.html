Zoom, Accumulate, Approximate

The three moves the whole subject is built from: zoom in until a curve goes straight (the derivative), add up infinitely many thin slivers (the integral), and stack polynomial terms until they grip any curve (Taylor series). Each one made visible.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Every Curve Is Secretly Straight

What's happening: the derivative at a point is just "how steep the curve is right there." To measure steepness you need a straight line — so we zoom in, because any smooth curve looks straight once you're close enough (the way the ground looks flat even though Earth is round).

Try this: drop the point anywhere and drag zoom all the way up. The curve flattens onto the gold tangent line, and the "curve-vs-tangent gap" reading below shrinks toward zero — the slope you measure is the derivative. Watch the dashed secant (slope across the whole window) slide onto the tangent as you close in.

The Fundamental Theorem, Live

What's happening: the top curve is f(x); the shaded region is the area under it so far, from 0 up to a sweeping x. The bottom curve, F(x), plots that running total of area as x moves — an "area-so-far" odometer.

Try this: let the sweep run (or drag x) and compare the two live numbers: the height of the top curve and the slope of the bottom curve. They stay identical, always. Where f is tall, the area piles up fast, so F climbs steeply; where f dips to zero, F goes flat.

0.0
Why it matters: nudge x by a sliver dx and you tack on one thin rectangle of area f(x)·dx — so the running total climbs at rate f(x). That's F′(x) = f(x): the slope of the accumulation equals the height of the curve, which is exactly why differentiating and integrating undo each other.

Building a Curve From Its DNA

What's happening: a Taylor series rebuilds an entire curve using only the derivatives of a single point (here, x = 0). The 0th derivative (the height itself) gives a flat line; the 1st derivative (slope) tilts it into a tangent line; the 2nd derivative (curvature) bends that line into a parabola; the 3rd derivative and beyond add steeper, lopsided wiggles — each one layered on to match the true curve ever more closely.

Try this: start at order 0 and press + order repeatedly. The gold dashed curve is only the newest derivative's own contribution — watch it shrink toward a flat zero line near the center (small x raised to a high power is tiny) while the purple cumulative polynomial reaches out and grips the true curve, widening the shaded grip zone. For sin and cos, watch some orders contribute a gold line that's exactly zero everywhere — a real, verifiable fact: sin's even derivatives and cos's odd derivatives are all zero at x = 0.

Why it matters: more terms widen the grip zone outward. Near the center the first few terms already win (high powers of a small x are tiny); far from center, the high-power terms roar in and do the heavy lifting. Every coefficient here is exactly f⁽ᵏ⁾(0)/k! — the k-th derivative at the center, divided by k factorial — nothing hand-fitted, nothing approximated.

Differentiate a Vector, Get an Arrow That Points Where You're Going

What's happening: the moving dot's position is a vector-valued function `r(t) = (x(t), y(t))` — one arrow per instant, tip tracing the path. Differentiate each coordinate separately (nothing new needed) and you get the velocity vector `r'(t)`, drawn from the moving dot — it always points in the exact direction of travel, and its length is the speed.

Try this: load "Circular motion" and scrub t — the velocity arrow (gold) stays perpendicular to the position arrow (thin purple) at every instant, never speeding up or slowing down. Then load "Projectile motion" and watch the *same* velocity arrow rotate from pointing up-and-forward to down-and-forward as gravity's constant pull (the acceleration vector, always straight down) steadily bends it — the parabola, and its one turning point, are what that steady bending looks like traced out.

Why it matters: this is the direct answer to "how are vectors related to calculus" for motion specifically — a position is a vector, and calculus differentiates it exactly the way it differentiates a number: one coordinate at a time. Robot-arm trajectories, game-character paths, and orbital mechanics are all this exact idea, just with a fancier `r(t)`.

Rate Ratios Are Just Size Ratios, Meshed Together

What's happening: three gears mesh in a row, A–B–C. Meshing forces equal rim speed at every point of contact, so each gear's spin rate is fixed by nothing but its radius relative to its neighbor's. The highlighted tooth on each gear makes the speed difference visible as they spin.

Try this: hit the tutorial preset and watch A spin exactly 3× as fast as B, and B exactly 2× as fast as C — then watch A spin 6× as fast as C, with the middle radius canceling out of the math live below. Drag any radius and watch every ratio update immediately, including which way each gear turns.
