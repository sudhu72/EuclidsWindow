Six Sharp Tools

Trace and determinant, matrix norms, Cauchy–Schwarz, similar matrices and the Jordan form, conjugate gradient, and the Kronecker product. Every worked example from the file is a one-click preset that plays itself: use Step to go one move at a time, Play to watch, or Jump to end for the answer. Every number is computed live by this page. There's nothing to type.

The diagonal already knows the eigenvalues' sum and product

Each preset writes the characteristic polynomial, solves it, and checks the sum against the trace and the product against the determinant. The plot is the complex plane: eigenvalues are dots, and the diamond is their average, trace / n.

How big is a matrix?

The Frobenius norm measures the entries as one long list. The spectral norm measures the most the matrix can stretch any unit arrow. The condition number is the ratio of the most-stretched to the most-squashed direction.

A shadow can't be longer than the stick

|u·v| ≤ ‖u‖·‖v‖ in any number of dimensions. The proof: ‖u − t·v‖² is a parabola in t that can never go below zero.

Same transformation, different coordinates, and the matrices that can't be diagonalized

B = P⁻¹AP is A rewritten in the basis made of P's columns. When a matrix doesn't have enough eigenvectors, the best you can do is the Jordan form.

Conjugate gradient: directions that never undo each other

Solving Ax = b for a symmetric positive-definite A is finding the bottom of the bowl ½xᵀAx − bᵀx. The regression bowl is A = [[30, 10], [10, 5]], b = (38, 15), answer (0.8, 1.4).

The Kronecker product: a table of tables

A ⊗ B replaces every entry aᵢⱼ of A with the block aᵢⱼ·B. The vec trick (A ⊗ B)·vec(X) = vec(B·X·Aᵀ) means you almost never have to build it.
