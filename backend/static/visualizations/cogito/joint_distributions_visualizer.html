The Joint Table, Its Margins, and One Slice

What's happening: each cell is P(coffees and sleep). Adding across a row or down a column gives the totals in the margins: the marginal distributions. Keeping one row and rescaling it to add to 1 gives a conditional distribution.

Try this: click through the four buttons in order; each one is a worked example from Parts 1 and 2.

Why it matters: every conditional probability, every Bayes calculation, and every "is A linked to B?" question is a question about a joint table: its margins, its slices, and whether it equals the product of its margins.

Covariance: Which Quadrants Win?

What's happening: the cross-hairs go through the two means. Points in the teal quadrants (both above or both below their means) add positive products (X − μX)(Y − μY); points in the red quadrants add negative ones. Covariance is the average product; correlation divides out both standard deviations. The histograms on the top and right edges are the two marginals.

Try this: compare ρ = 0.8 with ρ = −0.8, then click Y = X²: a perfect relationship with correlation ≈ 0.

Why it matters: correlation is the most-quoted number in data analysis, and it only ever measures a straight-line trend. The U-shape preset is the reason to always look at the cloud.

Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y)

What's happening: two stocks, each with standard deviation 0.2 (variance 0.04). Left: the cloud of their yearly returns. Right: the histogram of the pair's total return X + Y. Below: the three pieces of the variance identity, with the covariance piece positive (stacking up) or negative (cancelling).

Try this: step from ρ = +1 down to ρ = −1 and watch the right-hand histogram narrow until it is a single spike. Then try the students' preset, which uses the same identity to find the regression slope.

Why it matters: diversification, portfolio risk wTΣw, error propagation in engineering, and the bias–variance algebra are all this one squared bracket.

The Multivariate Normal, Built From a Round Hill

What's happening: start with two independent standard normals Z (a round hill), push them through a matrix A to get X = A Z with covariance Σ = A AT, and read the density by undoing the map: squared distance becomes (x − μ)TΣ−1(x − μ) and the height is divided by √det Σ. Ellipses are contours at Mahalanobis distance 1, 2 and 3.

Try this: go through the buttons left to right: they follow Part 5 step by step.

Why it matters: this density, with its Σ−1 and √det Σ, is the scoring rule inside LDA, QDA and every Gaussian mixture model, and its conditional slice is the regression line and the Kalman filter update.
