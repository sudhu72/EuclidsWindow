Trees, Goldfish, and the Long Run

Multiply down a path, add across paths — then let the same rule repeat forever and watch it forget where it started. Three hands-on tools for the one idea behind Bayes' theorem, PageRank, and predictive text.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

The Test That Fools Doctors

What's happening: a rare disease, a very accurate test, and a positive result. To find the chance you're actually sick, we send 10,000 imaginary people down the tree — first split by who's truly sick, then by who tests positive. The answer is the sick-and-positive group as a share of everyone who tested positive.

Try this: keep the test excellent (99% / 95%) but drag prevalence down to ~1%. P(sick | positive) collapses to about 1-in-6 — because false alarms from the huge healthy majority far outnumber the few real cases. Slide prevalence back up and watch the number climb toward what your gut expected.

Why it matters: multiply down each path to get its leaf, add the two positive leaves to get everyone who tested positive, then take part ÷ whole. That flips "how accurate is the test?" into the question you actually care about — "given a positive, am I sick?" — which is Bayes' theorem. The tree is the theorem.

Why "HH" Takes Longer Than "HT"

What's happening: two independent fair-coin streams, each hunting its own two-flip pattern — one watches for HH, the other for HT. Both patterns are equally likely on any given pair of flips, so it feels like they should take the same time to show up. They don't.

Try this: press Watch one live race a few times and see HH's stream often run noticeably longer. Then press Run 1,000 more instantly repeatedly — the running averages settle onto 6 flips for HH and 4 flips for HT, exactly the numbers the Polya worked example derives from scratch.

Absorbing States: When the Story Only Ends One of Two Ways

What's happening: you start with some money, bet $1 on a fair coin every round, and stop the moment you either go broke ($0) or hit your target. $0 and the target are absorbing states — once you land there, the story is over.

Try this: drag your starting bankroll and watch the theoretical win-chance track it exactly — your share of the total pot, nothing more exotic. Then Run one walk to watch a single bankroll wander up and down before finally hitting an edge, and Run 1,000 walks to confirm the empirical win rate lands on that same simple fraction.

How Much History Actually Belongs in "the State"?

What's happening: a real weather sequence was generated where tomorrow's rain chance genuinely depends on both today and yesterday — not just today. Pick how much history you're willing to pack into "the state" and see whether that's actually enough to predict well.

Try this: start with "today only." Its predictions look plausible but are secretly a blend of two very different true rates. Switch to "today + yesterday" and watch the table split apart into the correct, verified numbers — then check the italic comparison row: going one day further back barely moves the number at all, which is exactly how you'd confirm a state is sufficient instead of just guessing.

Why it matters: this is the exact design question behind an AI agent's context window — the model can only ever see its current state (whatever's in context), so the real question is never "is this Markovian" (a bounded context forces that), it's "does this state pack in enough history to be sufficient" — precisely what this tab lets you check by hand.

When Does a Chain Settle, and How Fast?

What's happening: each preset is a small chain and a starting belief. Press Step to push the belief forward one step, Play to animate it, or Jump to step 60 to go straight to the end. The black ticks show where the belief should end up, if it settles at all.

Try this: start with Healthy weather chain: it settles. Then try Flip chain: it bounces forever. The closer the second eigenvalue is to 1, the longer the chain takes to forget its start.

Why it matters: a chain settles on one answer, whatever its start, only when it is irreducible (every state can reach every other), aperiodic (it does not cycle on a fixed beat) and positive recurrent (it comes back to each state in finite average time). Each preset breaks or keeps one of these, and the second eigenvalue says how fast the settling happens.

The Random Surfer, With and Without Teleportation

What's happening: a tiny web of six pages. The surfer follows a random link with probability d. With probability 1 − d they get bored and jump to a random page. F has no outgoing links (a dangling node). D and E link only to each other (a spider trap). Each step pushes the whole crowd of surfers forward once. This is power iteration.

Try this: press d = 1, raw links and then Jump. Total mass leaks out through F, and what is left sloshes between D and E forever. Press d = 1, dangling fixed: no more leak, but it still sloshes. Now press d = 0.85 and Jump. It settles in 99 steps, exactly on the black ticks. The ticks come from a direct linear solve, a separate method from stepping.

Why it matters: teleportation gives every page a small direct link to every other page. That one change makes the chain irreducible and aperiodic, so a single stationary distribution is guaranteed and power iteration must reach it. The error shrinks by at least a factor of d each step, so a smaller d settles faster but listens less to the real link structure.

The Dishonest Casino: Seeing Rolls, Guessing Dice

What's happening: a casino secretly switches between a Fair die and a Loaded die. The loaded die rolls a 6 half the time. You see only the rolls. Fair stays fair 95% of the time and Loaded stays loaded 90% of the time. You walk in at a random moment, so your starting belief is 2/3 Fair, 1/3 Loaded. Press Step to fill one column of the trellis (one roll), Play to animate, or Jump to end.

Try this: pick the 16 rolls preset and Jump to end. The forward rows (sum over all paths) and the Viterbi rows (best single path) fill in. Then press Brute-force check: it tries all 65,536 hidden paths one by one and gets the same two numbers. Look for roll 13, where the single best path and the best guess for each roll on its own disagree.

Baum–Welch: learning the dice from rolls alone

3,000 rolls are generated from the true casino (fixed seed 7). Baum–Welch is not told the true numbers. It starts from a rough guess (stay 0.8, loaded six 0.25) and runs forward–backward, then re-estimates, again and again. Each round's score, the log-likelihood, never goes down.

Why it matters: this exact machinery once decoded speech (hidden phonemes, observed sound) and still finds genes (hidden coding or non-coding regions, observed DNA letters). Forward gives the chance of the whole observation. Viterbi gives the single best hidden story. Forward–backward gives the chance of each hidden state at each moment. Baum–Welch learns the model's numbers when nobody labelled the hidden states.
