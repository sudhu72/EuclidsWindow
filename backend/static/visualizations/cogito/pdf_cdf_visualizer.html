PDFs and CDFs, Made Visible

Four verified demos: watch a density's area-so-far trace a live CDF; build uniform and exponential distributions from their sliders; watch the normal distribution's CDF come from the error function, not a shortcut; and watch inverse-CDF sampling turn plain uniform randomness into any shape you like.

Drag a Point, Watch the Area Build the CDF

What's happening: the top curve is a standard normal density f(x); the shaded region is the area swept from the far left up to your dragged point. The bottom curve is F(x) — the same running area, traced live as you drag.

Try this: drag to x=1.5 and confirm the readout matches the tutorial's verified F(1.5)=0.933193. Drag to x=0 and confirm F(0)=0.5 exactly, by the normal curve's symmetry.

Build a Flat Density, or a Decaying One

What's happening: switch between uniform and exponential, drag their parameters, and watch both f(x) and F(x) update live alongside a probability-range readout.

Try this: on Uniform[2,8], confirm F(5)=0.5 exactly. Switch to Exponential with λ=0.5 and confirm F(2)≈0.6321 — not 0.5 — because of the long right tail.

Building Φ(x) From the Error Function, Not a Shortcut

What's happening: drag x and watch three numbers agree: the normal CDF via the closed-form erf formula, the same value via brute-force numerical integration of the density, and the numerical derivative of the CDF (which should land back on the density itself).

Try this: confirm all three panels agree to 4+ decimal places at every x you drag to — this is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus holding exactly, not approximately.

Turn Plain Randomness Into Any Shape

What's happening: drag a uniform random number on the y-axis, watch it bounce off the live CDF curve, and land on the x-axis at exactly the value that draw produces. Release a flood of draws and watch the resulting histogram trace the original density.

Try this: release 5,000 draws and confirm the histogram's shape visibly matches the exponential density curve overlaid on top, and that the empirical P(1≤X≤3) lands close to the tutorial's verified 0.3834.
