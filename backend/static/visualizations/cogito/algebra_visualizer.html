Restoring the Balance

Five moves everyone learns and almost nobody watches happen: why solving an equation is literally "restoring a balance" (the word algebra's own meaning), why one rectangle proves the distributive property, why completing a square hands you the quadratic formula for free, how factoring is a searchable puzzle, and what a function actually does. Each one built from a picture, not asserted.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Solve It By Keeping Both Pans Level

What's happening: the equation is a balance scale. Each step below applies one legal move to both pans at once — the only kind of move a level scale allows — until the left pan holds nothing but x alone.

Try this: step through a preset equation and watch the scale stay level after every single move — that's not a coincidence, it's the definition of a legal algebraic step. Then check the final answer by substituting it back into the original equation.

Why it matters: "restoration" — moving a piece from one side to the other while keeping everything balanced — is the literal 9th-century meaning of the word "algebra."

One Rectangle, Counted Two Ways

What's happening: a single rectangle of height a and width (b+c) is sliced by one vertical line into two smaller rectangles, widths b and c. Its total area is a(b+c) — and, counted as two pieces, it's also ab + ac.

Try this: drag a, b, and c and watch both area readouts track each other exactly, every time — the distributive property isn't a rule to trust, it's this one rectangle refusing to have two different total areas.

Finish the Square With Its Missing Corner

What's happening: x² + bx looks like a complete x-by-x square with a b-wide strip bolted on. Split that strip in half, wrap the two halves around two adjacent sides, and exactly one small corner piece — area (b/2)² — is missing to finish a genuine, larger square.

Try this: drag x and b, then press Add the missing corner and watch the outline snap into one perfect square of side (x + b/2) — with the area tally confirming x² + bx + (b/2)² = (x+b/2)² exactly, every time.

Find the Two Numbers Doing Both Jobs

What's happening: factoring x² + Bx + C means finding two numbers that multiply to C and add to B, simultaneously. Pick a target quadratic, then search for the pair.

Try this: pick a target, then drag p and q until both checks turn green at once — that's the entire "factoring algorithm," a small searchable puzzle rather than a memorized procedure.

Feed It a Number, It Hands One Back

What's happening: a function is a deterministic machine. Sweep the input and watch the output trace out the graph, one point at a time — a straight line for a linear machine, a parabola with one clean turning point for a quadratic one.

Try this: switch to the quadratic machine and sweep the input past the vertex — the marked turning point sits exactly at x = −b/2a, the same point completing the square was built to find.
