Cause or Coincidence?

A treatment that wins in every subgroup and loses overall, the outcome you can never see, a drug that looks harmful only because doctors give it to the sickest patients, a coin flip that fixes all of it, and three little arrow diagrams that tell you when adjusting helps and when it quietly hurts. Every number comes from a fixed-seed simulation, so each click shows exactly the numbers in the doc.

Each panel reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this, and why it matters. Every worked example is one button.

The Kidney-Stone Reversal

What's happening: the real 1986 kidney-stone data (Charig et al.). Treatment A was open surgery, Treatment B a less invasive keyhole procedure. Each button shows the same 700 patients sliced a different way.

Try this: press "Combined" (B looks better), then "Split by stone size" (A wins both), then "Who got which treatment?" to see the reason, and finally "Adjusted" for the fair comparison.

Why it matters: stone size is a confounder: it pushed doctors toward a treatment and changed the odds of success. The combined numbers mix the treatment's effect with the case mix it was handed.

Two Futures per Patient, and You Only Get to See One

What's happening: the doc's simulated hospital (10,000 patients, fixed seed 11). Each patient has two potential outcomes: Y(1), recovered if treated, and Y(0), recovered if not. The simulation knows both; reality shows you only the one matching the treatment they actually got.

Try this: press "What the hospital sees" (half of every row is a question mark), then "God's-eye view" to reveal the missing column. Patients #23 and #33 are the only two in the first 40 for whom the treatment changes anything.

Why it matters: an individual causal effect is Y(1) − Y(0), and one of those two numbers is always missing. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference. Every method in the doc is a way of filling in the missing column fairly, on average.

The Same 10,000 Patients, Two Ways of Choosing Who Gets Treated

What's happening: half the patients are severe cases. Without treatment, mild cases recover 80% of the time and severe cases 30%. The treatment adds exactly +10 percentage points for everyone: that's the known true effect. Only who gets treated changes between buttons.

Try this: "Confounded" (doctors treat the sickest: the treatment looks harmful), then "Randomized" (a coin flip decides: the truth comes back), then "Confounded, compared within severity", then "Repeat 200 times" to see that neither result was luck.

Why it matters: randomization doesn't remove severity, it makes severity unrelated to who got treated, so the two groups are alike in every way except the treatment. That's why an A/B test can measure causation and a dashboard of observational data can't, on its own.

Three Shapes of Arrow, Three Different Rules

What's happening: a DAG (directed acyclic graph) is just a diagram of what causes what: an arrow from X to Y means "X directly affects Y". Three tiny shapes cover most of the trouble. For each, the simulation shows what happens to the X-Y relationship when you do and don't "control for" the middle variable.

Try this: in the fork, adjusting fixes the answer; in the chain, adjusting erases a real effect; in the collider, adjusting (here, looking only at the people who got selected) creates a relationship out of nothing.

Why it matters: "control for everything you can measure" is wrong advice. Control for forks (common causes), don't control for chains if you want the total effect, and never control for colliders. The data alone can't tell you which shape you have; the causal story has to.
