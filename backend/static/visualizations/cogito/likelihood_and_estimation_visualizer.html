Where Every Loss Function Comes From

Likelihood holds the data fixed and asks how well each explanation fits it. Squared error, cross-entropy and regularization all fall out of that one idea. Every worked example from the file is a one-click preset below, and every number is computed live on this page.

7 heads in 10 flips: which coin explains it best?

One formula, C(10,7) · p⁷ · (1−p)³, read two ways. Hold the data fixed and slide p: that's the likelihood curve. Hold p fixed and vary the outcome: that's a probability distribution.

2,000 flips: the product dies, the log survives

2,000 flips with exactly 1,394 heads, evaluated at p = 0.7. Each flip contributes a factor of 0.7 (heads) or 0.3 (tails). Teal is the running log-likelihood (a sum of logs). Gold is the log of the running product as the computer actually stores it, until the stored product becomes exactly 0.

The coin, solved: the slope is zero at 0.7

For 7 heads in 10, log L(p) = 7·ln p + 3·ln(1−p) and its slope is 7/p − 3/(1−p). Uphill before the peak, flat at the peak, downhill after.

Three candidate lines, ranked two ways

Values are spread evenly between 0 and an unknown maximum θ. Data: 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 3.0. The shaded band is the range each estimate allows; a data point outside it could not have happened.

How sure is the estimate? Fisher information

A sharp log-likelihood peak pins the parameter down; a flat one doesn't. Sharpness is curvature, minus the second derivative at the peak, called the information. One over its square root is the standard error, and one over the information is the Cramér–Rao bound: the smallest variance any unbiased estimator can have.
