Companion to One's and Two's Complement

Negative Numbers With No Minus Sign

Each bit is a switch with a lamp: lit means 1, dark means 0. Every example is a one-click preset that plays itself; use Step to go one move at a time, Play to watch, or Jump to end to see the finished answer. There's nothing to type.

Same +3 and −3, same adder, three ways to store them

The adder never knows which scheme you picked. It just adds the patterns, right to left, carrying as it goes.

All 16 four-bit patterns on a clock face

Counting up goes clockwise, and after 1111 comes 0000. Two's complement just relabels the left half of the wheel as negatives.

how to read the patterns

Negate: flip every bit, then add 1

Flipping computes 15 − x (subtracting from all ones never borrows). Adding 1 makes it 16 − x, which is how two's complement stores −x.

One circuit adds signed and unsigned numbers alike

The adder does exactly the same thing whatever the bits "mean". Subtraction is the same adder with a row of inverters and a carry-in of 1.

When the wheel wraps the wrong way

Overflow happens exactly when both inputs have the same sign and the result has the opposite sign. The sign bits are ringed in red.

Widening 4 bits to 8: copy the sign bit

The new places on the left get copies of the sign bit. Padding with zeros instead only works for unsigned numbers.

RFC 1071's checksum, one word at a time

Add the 16-bit words; whenever a sum spills past 16 bits, wrap the carry around and add it back in at the bottom (one's complement's end-around carry). Finally flip every bit.
