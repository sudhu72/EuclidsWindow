What Refuses to Change

Symmetry sounds like it's about pretty, balanced shapes — but its real meaning is a verb: an action you can perform that changes nothing (spin a square 90°, mirror a face). And wherever you can change something without effect, a quantity is secretly locked in place — an invariant. These three toys let you feel that pairing: a counting trick that proves a puzzle can't be solved, a planet whose locked quantity you can watch on a dial, and a perfectly balanced ball forced to pick a side.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

The Counting Trick That Proves the Impossible

What's happening: a domino has a hidden rule — dropped anywhere, it always covers exactly one light square and one dark square. It literally can't do otherwise. So the count of leftover light vs. dark squares is something no move can cheat (that's the invariant). If you remove squares until light and dark no longer match, the rest can never be fully tiled — and you know it without trying a single arrangement.

Try this: click Remove opposite corners — they're the same color, leaving 32 vs 30 — and the counter turns red and calls the board impossible before you place one piece. Then switch to Place dominoes and see you always end up with two same-colored squares stranded apart.

Click any square to remove or restore it.
Why it matters: matched counts don't guarantee a tiling exists — but a mismatch is an airtight proof that it doesn't. Finding a quantity every move preserves, then showing your goal would have to break it, is exactly how mathematicians prove something is impossible instead of just failing to find it.

A Conservation Law You Can Watch

What's happening: a planet feels gravity pointing straight at the Sun. Because that setup looks the same no matter how you rotate it — no direction is special — a quantity called angular momentum gets frozen: it can't change. You can see this two ways at once. The dial holds a steady number, and the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times — racing when close to the Sun, dawdling when far, so every gold pie-slice comes out the same size.

Try this: watch the gold slices come out equal and the L reading hold steady (green). Then press Break the symmetry — now space has a preferred direction, like a steady sideways wind. The slices grow lopsided and L drifts (red). The symmetry and the frozen quantity live or die together.

Each bar = area the planet sweeps during one equal stretch of time. Equal bars = angular momentum conserved.
The big idea — Noether's theorem: every symmetry of the laws of nature hides a conserved quantity. Rotational symmetry ⟺ angular momentum. It rhymes elsewhere too: the laws being the same yesterday and today ⟺ energy is conserved; the same here and over there ⟺ momentum is conserved.

Perfectly Balanced — Until It Isn't

What's happening: the landscape is a perfect mirror image — the two valleys are identical and the peak in the middle favors neither side. A ball balanced exactly on the peak should be able to sit there forever. But that balance is unstable: the faintest wobble tips it into one valley, and which one is pure luck. The rules never choose a side — the outcome always does.

Try this: the ball starts on the peak. Give it a Nudge, or just wait — a microscopic jiggle is enough to tip it. Note which valley it lands in, then Reset and watch it possibly fall the other way. Same symmetric setup every time; different result.

Why it matters — this is spontaneous symmetry breaking: the same mechanism behind a cooling magnet choosing a north pole, the Higgs field giving particles their mass, and matter barely out-surviving antimatter so that anything exists at all. Structure is broken symmetry. Reset a few times — the choice is never predictable.

The Invariant Hiding in Modular Arithmetic

What's happening: whenever two differently-colored chameleons meet, both switch to the third color. Set starting counts for red, green, and blue, then let meetings happen. Watch the three counts change — and watch their differences mod 3, which never do.

Try this: with the default 13/15/17, run meetings for a while — the counts wander all over the place, but the mod-3 residues shown below stay frozen exactly where they started. Since they start all different, no two counts can ever become equal to each other, which is exactly what "all one color" would require. Then try 13/16/17 (two residues now match) and watch the verdict flip to possible.
