The Restoring-Force Equation: A Spring, Not a Drift

What's happening: the direction-field tab's equation, dx/dt = k·x, only ever grows or decays — it can't swing back and forth. A spring needs a different rule: the force always pulls back toward center, proportional to how far you've stretched it, d²x/dt² = −ω²x. Watch the mass bounce while the trace below draws out x(t) in real time.

Try this: raise ω and watch the spring oscillate faster — a stiffer spring restores itself quicker. Then raise the damping b above zero and watch the once-endless swing decay into stillness, exactly the way a real pendulum eventually settles.

Where this connects: the eigenvector directions here are the same "stretch, don't rotate" idea from linear algebra — try the shear preset and notice only one direction survives. The span builder is the same tutorial's other core idea, one level more basic than eigenvectors: not "which direction survives," but "which points are reachable at all" by scaling and adding two vectors. The tangent line is calculus's "local straight-line approximation," and the Riemann sum is literally the construction behind the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The direction field traces exactly the "leaf on a river of arrows" picture from differential equations — Euler's method is that picture, made computational, one tiny step at a time — and the oscillator is that same differential-equations toolkit applied to the one shape (restoring force) a pure growth/decay equation can never produce.
