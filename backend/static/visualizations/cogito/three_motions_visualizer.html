Straightening Two Vectors Into a Perpendicular Pair

What's happening: two vectors v₁ and v₂ start out pointing in whatever directions you give them. Gram-Schmidt builds an orthonormal pair q₁, q₂ one at a time: q₁ is just v₁ rescaled to length 1. Then it subtracts v₂'s shadow onto q₁ (the dashed grey projection) — the perpendicular leftover (dashed purple) gets rescaled to length 1 to become q₂. Those two lengths, plus that one shadow, are exactly the three numbers in R.

Try this: press Nearly parallel and watch how thin the leftover perpendicular sliver is — R's off-diagonal entry (the shadow length) is nearly as large as v₂ itself, exactly the unstable case QR is used to avoid. Then press Already perpendicular and watch the shadow collapse to zero — R becomes purely diagonal, and Q is just v₁, v₂ rescaled.

The Restoring-Force Equation: A Spring, Not a Drift

What's happening: the direction-field tab's equation, dx/dt = k·x, only ever grows or decays — it can't swing back and forth. A spring needs a different rule: the force always pulls back toward center, proportional to how far you've stretched it, d²x/dt² = −ω²x. Watch the mass bounce while the trace below draws out x(t) in real time.

By Parts and Substitution: Derivative Rules, Run Backward

What's happening: integration by parts is the product rule run backward, ∫u dv = [u·v] − ∫v du. Its picture is a rectangle split by a curve into two regions. Substitution is the chain rule run backward, ∫f(g(x))·g'(x)dx = ∫f(u)du: the same slivers, re-measured along a new axis. Every number below is computed live by numerical integration.

Try this: press each preset in turn. For the by-parts presets, check that the teal and gold areas add up to the big rectangle minus the small corner. For Exponential mean, drag the cutoff and watch the boundary toll shrink to 0. For substitution, check that the two different-looking curves enclose the same area.

Why it matters: there's no forward "product rule" for integrals. Instead, each derivative rule, read backward, is an integration rule. These two cover a huge share of the integrals in probability and machine learning, from means and variances to standardizing a bell curve.

Where this connects: the eigenvector directions here are the same "stretch, don't rotate" idea from linear algebra — try the shear preset and notice only one direction survives. The span builder is the same tutorial's other core idea, one level more basic than eigenvectors: not "which direction survives," but "which points are reachable at all" by scaling and adding two vectors. The tangent line is calculus's "local straight-line approximation," and the Riemann sum is literally the construction behind the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The direction field traces exactly the "leaf on a river of arrows" picture from differential equations — Euler's method is that picture, made computational, one tiny step at a time — and the oscillator is that same differential-equations toolkit applied to the one shape (restoring force) a pure growth/decay equation can never produce.
