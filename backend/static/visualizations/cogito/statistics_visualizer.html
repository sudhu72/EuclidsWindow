Reasoning Backward From Data

A billionaire dragging an average around while the median stands still, a pegboard that turns pure randomness into a bell curve, a sampling distribution that settles down no matter how lopsided the population is, a p-value computed live, and a trend that reverses the moment you combine two groups. Each one built from a picture, not asserted.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Mean, Median, and the Billionaire in the Bar

What's happening: pick a dataset shape and watch where the mean (accent), median (teal), and mode (gold) land — on symmetric data they huddle together; on skewed data they scatter.

Try this: switch to "Skewed (income-like)" and watch the mean get dragged toward the long tail while the median barely moves — the exact reason income is reported as a median.

Bessel's Correction, Caught in the Act

What's happening: draw repeated small samples (n=5) from a population with a known true variance of 4. Watch the naive "divide by n" estimate average out too low, while "divide by n−1" lands on target.

Why it matters: every reported "sample standard deviation" in a study, poll, or lab report is quietly using n−1 for exactly this reason.

Skewness & Kurtosis — the Shape Variance Can't See

What's happening: the exact ten bar patrons from above. Add the billionaire and watch skewness jump from roughly symmetric to strongly positive — the same event that dragged the mean into the millions, now given an actual number.

What's happening: eighteen calm days plus, optionally, two real crash days -- built to share the identical mean and standard deviation as a genuinely normal comparison set. Kurtosis is the number that tells the two apart when variance can't.

Why it matters: two datasets can share the identical mean and variance and still carry wildly different real-world risk -- kurtosis is the number 2008-era financial risk models left out.

A Bell Curve, Built From Nothing But Coin Flips

What's happening: each ball bounces left or right at every peg, purely at random, many rows deep. No single ball's path is predictable — but drop enough of them, and the bins at the bottom fill into an unmistakable bell curve.

Try this: drop balls one at a time and watch the pile look ragged and unpredictable; then drop hundreds at once and watch the ragged pile snap into a smooth, symmetric curve.

Why it matters: this is the Central Limit Theorem, physically enacted — sum enough small independent random effects and a bell curve appears, no matter the mechanism producing the individual effects.

Sample Means Settle Into a Bell Curve — Even From a Lopsided Population

What's happening: the population on the left is heavily skewed. Draw repeated samples of size n, compute each sample's mean, and watch the histogram of those means build up on the right.

Try this: draw a few hundred sample means at n=5 (still a bit lopsided), then switch to n=30 and draw again — the sample-mean histogram both centers on the true population mean and narrows, exactly as σ/√n predicts.

Why it matters: this is the exact machine underneath every confidence interval — it's what makes "how wrong could my sample mean be" a computable, honest question instead of a shrug.

Build a Confidence Interval, One Line of Arithmetic at a Time

What's happening: the recipe is interval = estimate ± (critical value) × SE, with SE = σ/√n. Each button below plays one fully worked scenario from the doc: the bell curve is where the sample mean would land if you redid the sampling, the shaded band is the interval, and every step of the arithmetic is written out underneath. No pen and paper needed.

Try this: start with "95% z-interval", then press "Double the sample" and "Quadruple the sample" — the faint dashed box stays behind as the original n=36 interval, so you can see doubling the data only trims about 29% while four times the data halves it.

sampling bell curve of x̄ (width = SE) the interval dashed: original n=36, 95% interval
Critical values used (from the doc's table): 90% two-sided 1.645 · 95% two-sided 1.960 · 99% two-sided 2.576 · 95% one-sided 1.645 · 80% power 0.842. t: df=30 → 2.042, df=35 → 2.030, df=100 → 1.984.

Does "95%" Actually Hold? Test It by Simulation

What's happening: each button draws thousands of fresh samples of n=36 from a population whose true mean is known, builds the t-interval x̄ ± 2.030 × s/√36 from each one, and counts how often the interval catches the truth. The chart shows the first 100 intervals: teal caught the true mean (vertical line), red missed it.

Try this: run the bell-shaped test (lands near 95%), then the skewed test — same recipe, same n, and the catch rate drops noticeably below 95%, with the misses piling up on one side.

interval contains the true mean interval misses it true mean
Press a coverage-test button to run the simulation.
Why it matters: the "95%" is a promise about the method over many repetitions, not about any one interval — and the promise leans on the Central Limit Theorem, so a strongly skewed population at modest n quietly breaks it. Simulations use a fixed-seed random generator, so every click reproduces the same result.

Could Pure Chance Alone Explain This?

What's happening: recreate Fisher's Lady Tasting Tea (8 cups, sorted into two groups of 4) or a coin-flip test. The bars show every possible outcome's chance under pure guessing; the p-value is the highlighted tail — how surprising your actual result would be if nothing real were going on.

Try this: in the tea test, set "correct" to 8 (all correct) and read off the classic 1/70 ≈ 1.4% p-value — then try 6 correct and watch the p-value grow, since a decent-but-imperfect result is far less surprising under pure guessing.

Why it matters: a p-value is never "the probability the null hypothesis is true" — it's "how likely this data would be, if the null hypothesis were true."

The Chi-Square Test, Run for Real

What's happening: each button loads one table of counts from the doc and runs the whole test, every line of arithmetic written out: the counts you'd expect if nothing were going on, how far each real count sits from its expected one, the total (the χ² statistic), the degrees of freedom, and the p-value. Top chart: solid bars are the real counts, dashed outlines are the expected counts, and the number under each pair is that cell's share of χ². Bottom chart: the chi-square curve for "nothing going on", with the p-value shaded in red.

Try this: start with "Three landing pages" (χ² = 7.80, p = 0.020). Then press "Same rates, 10× the visitors": the conversion rates are identical, but χ² jumps to 78 and the p-value collapses, while the effect size (Cramér's V) doesn't move at all.

observed count expected count if nothing is going on
Why it matters: the chi-square test only answers "are these counts too lopsided to blame on chance?" It never says which cell is responsible (read the per-cell shares for that), and with enough data it will flag even a tiny, useless difference.

One-Way ANOVA, Run for Real

What's happening: crop yields from three fertilizers, five plots each (the kind of field trial Fisher invented ANOVA for). Each dot is one plot; the short colored bar is that group's mean, the dashed line is the grand mean of all 15 plots. ANOVA asks: are the group means further apart than the plot-to-plot wobble inside each group would explain? Every step of the arithmetic is written out below the chart.

Try this: run "Three fertilizers" (F = 11.67, p = 0.0015), then "Same means, 3× the wobble": the three means haven't moved an inch, yet F drops to 1.30 and the result is no longer significant, because the noise inside the groups grew. Then press "Which pairs differ? (Tukey)" to see the follow-up question ANOVA itself can't answer.

Why it matters: a significant F says "at least one group mean differs", nothing more. Which ones, by how much, and whether it matters are separate questions (post-hoc tests, effect sizes, judgment).

Multiple Testing: Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg

What's happening: every test is another lottery ticket for a false positive. Bonferroni and Holm control the family-wise error rate (the chance of even one false discovery); Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate (the expected share of your discoveries that are false). The first four buttons apply each rule to a 10-metric A/B dashboard; the next four to 1,000 simulated tests where only the first 50 are real effects.

Try this: click through the four dashboard buttons and watch the bar change shape (flat, stepping up, a rising line). Then run "1,000 tests, 50 real: no correction" and count the red dots: 49 false positives, more than the 44 real effects found. Bonferroni removes every red dot, but also most of the real ones; BH keeps 19 real ones with a single red dot.

Why it matters: genome-wide studies use p < 5×10⁻⁸ (Bonferroni for a million tests); gene screens and experimentation dashboards report BH "q-values"; clinical trials must control the family-wise error across their endpoints.

Simpson's Paradox: A Trend That Reverses on Combining

What's happening: real 1986 kidney-stone treatment data. Treatment A wins in both subgroups shown separately — but Treatment B wins once the subgroups are combined.

Why it matters: Treatment A was used mostly on the harder large-stone cases — always check the subgroup breakdown before trusting a combined number.

How Many of These Numbers Are Actually Free to Move?

What's happening: five numbers, one fixed rule: their mean must stay exactly 6. Edit any of the first four freely — the fifth adjusts itself the instant you type, to keep the mean locked at 6. It has no freedom left; the other four already spent it.

Try this: set all four free values to 6 and watch the fifth land on exactly 6 too, no adjustment needed. Then push one free value far from 6 and watch the fifth swing hard the other way to compensate — it's never "off" by choice, it's the one value in the set with nothing left to decide.

—
Why it matters: this is exactly why sample variance divides by n−1, not n — the deviations from a sample's own mean must always sum to zero, so only n−1 of them were ever free to vary; the last one is bookkeeping, never new information.

ddof: One Parameter, Two Silent Defaults

What's happening: the divisor used to compute variance/std is always N − ddof. Drag the slider and watch the divisor, and the resulting standard deviation, change live on the same fixed dataset, [2, 4, 6, 8, 10].

Try this: set ddof to 0 and note the std — that's numpy's silent default. Now set it to 1 and note the std again — that's pandas' silent default. Same five numbers, two different "correct" answers, purely from an unannounced default.

0
Why it matters: numpy.std(x) defaults to ddof=0; pandas.Series.std() defaults to ddof=1 — mixing the two libraries on the same data without ever passing ddof explicitly silently computes two different numbers.
