The Toolkit, Made Visible

Five rules everyone uses and almost nobody re-derives: why multiplication runs before addition, why two negatives make a positive, why dividing by a fraction flips it, why an exponent can go all the way down to zero and past it, and why a 20-year table of logarithms once "doubled the life of the astronomer." Each one built from a picture or a pattern instead of asserted.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Watch an Expression Collapse, One Finishing Step at a Time

What's happening: type any expression using + − * / ^ ( ), or pick a preset. Each "Next step" collapses exactly one piece into a single number — always the piece that PEMDAS says must finish first, because it's building a unit something else needs.

Try this: step through the default expression and watch parentheses collapse first, then the exponent, then multiplication/division, and addition/subtraction only at the very end — never once a coincidence, always "this piece has to become one number before the next operation can touch it."

Why it matters: every one of these collapses is "build a same-unit quantity before combining it with anything else" — parentheses (explicit override) and exponents (repeated multiplication) and multiplication/division (repeated addition) all finish before addition/subtraction ever gets a turn.

Let the Pattern Force the Sign

What's happening: the top rows of this table (positive × negative) are ordinary, undisputed arithmetic. Reveal rows downward through zero and watch what the pattern itself demands the next rows must be — nobody has to tell you the sign, the unbroken straight line does.

Try this: reveal one row at a time and predict the next answer before it appears. The moment you cross 0 × (−2) = 0 going downward, the pattern insists the next row climbs back up into positive numbers — that forced climb is "negative times negative is positive."

Why it matters: this is the cleanest example in elementary math of a rule that doesn't need to be trusted on authority — refusing to let an already-true pattern break is a fully sufficient proof on its own.

Multiplying: The Overlap of Two Cuts

What's happening: shading a fraction of one direction, then a fraction of the other, leaves a double-shaded overlap — and that overlap's share of the whole rectangle is exactly the product of the two fractions.

Try this: set both fractions to simple values and count the grid cells yourself — the double-shaded count divided by the total cell count always matches the multiplied fraction, exactly.

Dividing: How Many of These Fit Inside That?

What's happening: a bar of length a gets chopped into chunks of size b/c. Count the chunks — that count is the division's answer, and it always matches flip-and-multiply exactly.

Try this: shrink the chunk size and watch the chunk count climb — dividing by a smaller fraction always gives a bigger answer, because more small things fit than big things do.

Why it matters: "flip and multiply" is never a separate rule — it's multiplying by a disguised 1 (a fraction's reciprocal times itself), legal at any time, for any number.

Concatenating Chains of Repeated Multiplication

What's happening: aᵘ is u copies of a multiplied together. Glue a chain of u copies to a chain of v copies, and you get one longer chain of u+v copies — which is exactly why exponents add when same-base powers multiply.

Try this: set u and v to small numbers and count the factors in the glued chain yourself — it always matches u+v.

Pushing the Pattern Past Zero

What's happening: reveal rows of aⁿ downward. Each step down divides by a — watch what that division-pattern forces once n reaches 0, then goes negative.

Try this: predict each new row before it appears — the pattern alone hands you a⁰=1 and "negative exponent = reciprocal," with no new rule required.

Why it matters: zero and negative exponents were never separate definitions to memorize — they're what the multiplication-chain pattern was always going to say, the moment you pushed it one step past where most people stop.

Napier and Briggs's Trick, Rebuilt: A Working Slide Rule

What's happening: the ruler below is marked by logarithm, not by value — the distance from the left edge to any mark is proportional to log₁₀(value). Slide the teal length for x1, then continue with the gold length for x2, laid end to end. The landing point is the product x1 × x2 — read off by adding two lengths, exactly the trick that let 17th-century astronomers avoid multiplying by hand.

Try this: set x1=2, x2=4 and check the landing point lands exactly on 8. Then try x1=2.5, x2=6 — even with non-whole numbers, adding the two log-lengths lands exactly on the product, every time, because that's all a logarithm ever was: the length that turns multiplying into adding.
