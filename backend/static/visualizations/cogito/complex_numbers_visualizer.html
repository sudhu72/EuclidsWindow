A Point, Not a Mystery

What's happening: drag the point. Its horizontal position is the "real part," its vertical position is the "imaginary part" — two ordinary coordinates, nothing more. The distance from the origin is its modulus; the angle from the positive real axis is its argument.

Try this: drag the point onto the horizontal axis — it's now an ordinary real number. Drag it onto the vertical axis — it's "purely imaginary," which just means "pointing straight up," nothing spookier.

Multiply Two Numbers, Watch the Angles Add

What's happening: set two complex numbers by their modulus (length) and argument (angle). Their product's length is the two lengths multiplied; its angle is the two angles added — plotted live, and checked against a direct FOIL multiplication.

Try this: set z₂'s modulus to 1 and its angle to 90° — multiplying by it rotates z₁ by a perfect quarter turn with no change in size. That's exactly what multiplying by i does.

The Calculation That Forced the Issue, in 1572

What's happening: x³ − 15x − 4 = 0 has a known, checkable real root: x=4. Watch what happens when you actually run Cardano's formula on it, step by step.

Try this: step all the way through and watch two "impossible" complex numbers, 2+11i's cube roots, add together and land exactly on the real answer you already knew was correct.

Why it matters: this is the actual historical reason complex numbers exist — not an abstract itch to solve x²=−1, but a real, verified answer that had no other known route.

The "Missing" Roots Were Never Missing

What's happening: zⁿ = 1 always has exactly n solutions once complex numbers are allowed — equally spaced points around the unit circle, since raising to the n-th power multiplies the angle by n, and landing back on angle 0 needs the starting angle to be an exact 1/n slice of the full circle.

Try this: set n=4 and see the four 4th roots of unity include 1 and −1 (the only two real roots of x⁴−1=0) plus i and −i — the two roots real numbers alone could never find.

4
Why it matters: this is the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, made visible — every one of the n roots is real and present, just not always on the real number line.

Every Polynomial's Roots, Found and Plotted

What's happening: a polynomial is just a sum of whole-number powers of `x`, each scaled by a coefficient — `a·x⁴ + b·x³ + c·x² + d·x + e` below. A root is any `x` that makes the whole expression equal 0 — where the curve would cross (or, for a complex root, "would cross if the real number line went far enough," which it never does). This panel finds every one of them — real or complex — using the same iterative "guess, then correct" idea as Newton's method, run on all the roots simultaneously, and plots every one on the complex plane.

Try this: load "x⁴−1" and compare against the Roots of Unity tab — same four points, `1, i, −1, −i`, found this time by generic root-hunting instead of the unit-circle shortcut. Then load "x²+x+1" and watch a plain quadratic (no `x³` or `x⁴` at all) produce two roots that never touch the real axis — exactly the "no real solution" case Part D of the Algebra Toolkit flags, now shown to have an answer after all, just not a real one.

n Runners on a Circular Track

What's happening: take the `n`-th roots of unity, raise each one to the `k`-th power, and walk them tip-to-tail in order — arrow 1 starts where the last one ended. This chains together exactly the sum `Σ(n-th roots of unity)ᵏ`, one step at a time, so you can watch whether it cancels or reinforces instead of just being told the answer.

Try this: load "n=3, k=1 (cancels)" and watch the walk trace a triangle that snaps shut exactly back at the start — zero net displacement. Then load "n=3, k=3 (reinforces)" and watch the exact same 3 steps instead line up into one straight line — no cancellation at all, because every angle collapsed to the same direction. "n=6, k=2" shows the prettier case: the walk still cancels to exactly zero, just by tracing two overlapping triangles instead of one hexagon.
