Multiply Down, Add Across

Two events branching into four outcomes, a forecaster graded on confidently-wrong beliefs, and one die rolled ten thousand times — three ways to feel the Operation-Choice Framework's "same unit adds, for-every multiplies" rule doing all the real work in probability.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Two Events, Four Outcomes, Two Operations

What's happening: event A and event B each either happen or don't. Multiplying down a path gives one leaf's probability (an AND); adding several leaves across gives an OR. Toggle whether B's odds depend on A to see conditional probability enter the same tree.

Try this: with "Independent" selected, set P(A)=50%, P(B)=50%, and click Highlight P(A or B) — watch it total 75%, not 100%, because the AB leaf would otherwise be double-counted. Then switch to "B depends on A" and push P(B|A) high while P(B|¬A) stays low — watch the tree stop looking symmetric, exactly what dependence means.

How Few People Does It Take?

What's happening: drag the room size and watch the exact probability that at least two people share a birthday — computed the honest way, one person at a time, not by the "count the pairs" shortcut. The simulation alongside confirms the formula by actually rolling thousands of random rooms.

Try this: set the room to 23 and run a batch of simulated rooms — watch the empirical match rate land right on the formula's 50.7%. Then compare 10 people against 50: the curve climbs far faster than "365 days in a year" makes it feel like it should.

23
Why it matters: the number of pairs of people grows quadratically as the room fills, while any one pair's own match chance stays fixed at a tiny 1/365 — quadratic growth against a fixed small chance is what makes "surprisingly few people" actually check out.

Pairs Explorer — Everyone Paired With Everyone, Two Ways to Count It

What's happening: is the birthday paradox about one person checked against everyone else, or about every possible two-person pairing? Step people into the room one at a time and watch: each new arrival draws a line to everyone already there (their "checks"), and the running total of lines always lands exactly on the batch handshake count n×(n−1)/2 — the same 253 pairs, discovered one arrival at a time instead of listed all at once.

Try this: set the room to 6 people, click "Reset" then "Next arrival" six times, and watch the running total climb 0 → 1 → 3 → 6 → 10 → 15, always matching the batch count shown alongside. Why it matters: the sequential arrival process and the "count all pairs at once" shortcut are never in tension — they're two orderings of the identical set of pairs, which is why both routes to the birthday paradox agree almost exactly on the final answer.

The Birthday Attack — the Same Math, Aimed at a Hash Function

What's happening: a hash function with an n-bit output has 2ⁿ possible "fingerprints" — the birthday room's 365 days, generalized. Drag the bit-length and compare two very different attacks: finding any two inputs that collide (the birthday-paradox effect) versus finding one specific input matching a fingerprint you already have.

Try this: set bits to 8 and read both attempt counts — collision-finding needs only ~20 tries, specific-matching needs ~178. Now drag bits up toward 24 or 32 and watch the ratio between them explode — that widening gap is exactly why real hash functions double their output length instead of just matching the target security level.

8
Why it matters: collision search only ever needs on the order of √N attempts, while matching one specific target needs on the order of N attempts — so making collisions as hard to find as a specific match requires squaring N, i.e. doubling the bit-length, which is exactly why SHA-256 outputs 256 bits rather than the 128 a naive "128-bit security" target would suggest.

Grading a Forecaster's Honesty

What's happening: the true chance of rain is p; the forecaster's stated belief is q. Cross-entropy H(p,q) is the average surprise you'd feel living through many days under the true odds while trusting the forecaster's belief — and it's always at least as large as the best-possible H(p), the surprise you'd feel if the forecaster were perfectly honest (q=p). The gap between them, H(p,q) − H(p), is the KL divergence — literally the extra average surprise you're paying for the forecaster's dishonesty, on top of the surprise you'd pay anyway even with a perfect belief. Read it live in the verdict box below: it reports H(p,q), H(p), and that gap all at once.

Try this: set true rain-chance p to 90% and slide the forecaster's belief q down toward 10% — watch H(p,q) shoot upward while H(p) stays put. That's the cost of confident wrongness. Now set q = p exactly and watch the gap collapse to zero.

One Die, Rolled Ten Thousand Times

What's happening: a single die roll is anyone's guess — 1 through 6, no pattern. But track the running average as more rolls pile up, and watch it clamp down onto 3.5, the true expectation, even though no single roll can ever land there.

Try this: press Roll 1 a few times and watch the average swing wildly. Then press Roll 1,000 more repeatedly — the wilder swings vanish, and the trace hugs the dashed 3.5 line tighter with every batch. That narrowing, not any single roll, is what makes probability practically useful.

Why it matters: this single fact — averages of independent samples clamp onto the true expectation — is why insurance companies, casinos, and pollsters can be supremely confident about a crowd's behavior while being unable to predict any one individual roll, customer, or voter.

Average of the Outputs vs. Output at the Average

What's happening: for a curve that bends upward (convex, like x²), the average of the outputs is at least the output at the average: E[f(X)] ≥ f(E[X]). For a curve that bends downward (concave, like ln x) it flips. The picture: join the points on the curve with a straight chord. Averaging the outputs means standing on the chord; plugging in the average means standing on the curve.

Try this: each button below plays one fully worked example from the tutorial, every number computed live. Start with x² with X = 1 or 9 and read the gap of 16 off the red segment, then click ln x with X = 1 or 9 and watch the chord drop below the curve.

Why it matters: Jensen is the one rule behind variance ≥ 0, the geometric mean ≤ the arithmetic mean, KL ≥ 0, the EM algorithm never lowering its likelihood, and the ELBO. It is also the "flaw of averages": feed an average input into a curved formula and you do not get the average output.

Break a Hard Average Into Easy Pieces

What's happening: pretend you know one extra fact (which factory, which class, how many coins), solve the easy problem inside each case, then average the answers, weighting each case by how likely it is. That's the law of total probability, the law of total expectation (tower rule, E[X] = E[E[X | Y]]), and the law of total variance (Var X = E[Var(X|Y)] + Var(E[X|Y])).

Try this: each button plays one worked example from §3.5 with every number computed live. Start with Three factories, then Die then coins and Law of total variance, which use the same game.

Why it matters: the Markov-chain "expected steps" equations, RL's Bellman equation, ANOVA and R², compound insurance claims, and Bayesian hierarchical models are all these three laws.

Guarantees From Almost Nothing

What's happening: Markov's inequality, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a for X ≥ 0, needs only the mean. Chebyshev's, P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k², is Markov applied to (X − μ)². Hoeffding is Markov applied to an exponential. Red is always the guaranteed bound; the other colours are what real distributions actually do.

Try this: each button plays one worked example from §3.6. Start with the income analogy, then see how loose the bound is on real distributions, then the one case where it's exact, and finish with the ladder from Markov to Hoeffding.

Why it matters: tail-latency guarantees from an average, distribution-free control limits, the proof of the Law of Large Numbers, and (via Hoeffding) every generalization bound in learning theory.
