Multiply Down, Add Across

Two events branching into four outcomes, a forecaster graded on confidently-wrong beliefs, and one die rolled ten thousand times — three ways to feel the Operation-Choice Framework's "same unit adds, for-every multiplies" rule doing all the real work in probability.

Each panel below reads top to bottom: what's happening, a Try this to poke at, and why it matters.

Two Events, Four Outcomes, Two Operations

What's happening: event A and event B each either happen or don't. Multiplying down a path gives one leaf's probability (an AND); adding several leaves across gives an OR. Toggle whether B's odds depend on A to see conditional probability enter the same tree.

Try this: with "Independent" selected, set P(A)=50%, P(B)=50%, and click Highlight P(A or B) — watch it total 75%, not 100%, because the AB leaf would otherwise be double-counted. Then switch to "B depends on A" and push P(B|A) high while P(B|¬A) stays low — watch the tree stop looking symmetric, exactly what dependence means.

Why it matters: every probability formula you'll meet is one of these two moves — multiply down a single path (AND, an independent-events "for every" scaling), or add across several non-overlapping paths (OR) — occasionally with a double-counted overlap subtracted back out once.

How Few People Does It Take?

What's happening: drag the room size and watch the exact probability that at least two people share a birthday — computed the honest way, one person at a time, not by the "count the pairs" shortcut. The simulation alongside confirms the formula by actually rolling thousands of random rooms.

Try this: set the room to 23 and run a batch of simulated rooms — watch the empirical match rate land right on the formula's 50.7%. Then compare 10 people against 50: the curve climbs far faster than "365 days in a year" makes it feel like it should.

23
Why it matters: the number of pairs of people grows quadratically as the room fills, while any one pair's own match chance stays fixed at a tiny 1/365 — quadratic growth against a fixed small chance is what makes "surprisingly few people" actually check out.

Grading a Forecaster's Honesty

What's happening: the true chance of rain is p; the forecaster's stated belief is q. Cross-entropy H(p,q) is the average surprise you'd feel living through many days under the true odds while trusting the forecaster's belief — and it's always at least as large as the best-possible H(p), the gap being the KL divergence.

Try this: set true rain-chance p to 90% and slide the forecaster's belief q down toward 10% — watch H(p,q) shoot upward while H(p) stays put. That's the cost of confident wrongness. Now set q = p exactly and watch the gap collapse to zero.

Why it matters: H(p,q) ≥ H(p) always, with equality only when q=p — this is precisely why training a model to minimize cross-entropy pushes its beliefs toward the truth, never away from it.

One Die, Rolled Ten Thousand Times

What's happening: a single die roll is anyone's guess — 1 through 6, no pattern. But track the running average as more rolls pile up, and watch it clamp down onto 3.5, the true expectation, even though no single roll can ever land there.

Try this: press Roll 1 a few times and watch the average swing wildly. Then press Roll 1,000 more repeatedly — the wilder swings vanish, and the trace hugs the dashed 3.5 line tighter with every batch. That narrowing, not any single roll, is what makes probability practically useful.

Why it matters: this single fact — averages of independent samples clamp onto the true expectation — is why insurance companies, casinos, and pollsters can be supremely confident about a crowd's behavior while being unable to predict any one individual roll, customer, or voter.
